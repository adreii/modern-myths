## 1. Where the idea comes from
Start with an everyday thing kids live with: likes, followers, grades, being picked first, brand shoes, "main character energy", being the fastest, winning arguments, saying sorry, birthdays, group chats, screen time, being "mature".

Ask Barthes's question: what does this thing seem to say, and what does it quietly make us believe is natural? A like seems to say "people enjoyed this"; it quietly teaches "visible approval is the measure of worth". That hidden belief is the myth.

Then make the myth **literal and physical** in a Roblox world. That move is the whole engine of the channel:
- worth as visible approval → a number floating over every head
- popularity → a town where the sun only shines on whoever is most liked
- apologies feel like losing → apologies literally cost coins, and the hero is broke
- being "cool" means not caring → cool kids slowly turn transparent
- winning is everything → every argument ends with a trophy appearing; houses fill with trophies and empty of friends

## 2. The absurdist toolbox
Absurd humour works when one impossible thing is treated with complete logic and seriousness. Tools:
- **Literalised metaphor.** Take a saying at face value ("carry a grudge" → the hero drags a growing boulder around the map).
- **Deadpan rule.** The narrator states the impossible as boring fact. Never wink, never "lol so random".
- **Logical escalation.** Follow the rule's consequences one step further than anyone would (if apologies cost money, there are apology loans, apology banks, an apology billionaire who has never said sorry).
- **Rule of three, then break it.** Two normal beats, the third goes sideways. Or three escalating beats, then a fourth tiny one that undercuts them.
- **Scale contrast.** Enormous reactions to small things, tiny reactions to enormous things.
- **Sudden cut.** A serious line, then a hard cut to a silly picture that answers it.
- **Callback.** A throwaway joke from the first 20 seconds returns at the turn and suddenly means something.
- **Gentle humbling.** The confident, cool or bossy character gets deflated, softly, and is never humiliated.
- **Background gags.** Something odd happening at the edge of frame for rewatchers (an NPC also slowly chasing a number).

Influences worth borrowing tone from: Aesop and La Fontaine (animal logic, short shape), Arnold Lobel's *Frog and Toad* and *Fables* (gentle absurdity, one-line twist morals), Roald Dahl (the comeuppance of the smug), Shel Silverstein (dark-ish whimsy, flipped endings), Bluey (adults and kids taken seriously, quiet emotional turns), Adventure Time (rules of a strange world played straight), Wes Anderson (symmetrical deadpan staging).

Keep it kind: jokes are about ideas, rules and situations. Never about bodies, disabilities, accents, families, money at home, or any group.

## 3. Twist patterns for the lesson
The lesson should arrive like a punchline: surprising at first, obvious a second later.
- **The measure was wrong.** The thing everyone counted never counted what mattered (EP01).
- **The quiet one was the hero.** A minor background character turns out to be doing the real good the whole time.
- **The wish granted.** The hero gets exactly what the myth promised, and it's empty.
- **The villain's reason.** The annoying character had a reason we didn't see; kindness was the missing information.
- **The inversion.** The myth is right, but about the wrong person (the "loser" was winning at something nobody measured).
- **The cost moves.** Avoiding a small cost (sorry, sharing) creates a huge silly cost later.
- **The audience is implicated.** The last shot turns the rule onto the viewer: their phone, their number, their choice.

Avoid: "and the lesson is…", any line that names the virtue ("so be kind!"), a character learning by being punished harshly, endings where the good person is rewarded with more of the myth (more likes for being kind defeats the point).

## 4. Comic timing in 60 to 90 seconds
- One laugh or surprise at least every 10 to 15 seconds; Shorts viewers swipe on any lull.
- Put the setup in the voice and the punchline in the picture (or the reverse). Don't let both say the same thing.
- Hold a beat of silence before a punchline image; cut out of it fast.
- The turn is the one place to slow down. Music out, one held shot, fewer words. Contrast with the fast escalation is what makes it hit.
- End within 3 seconds of the landing line. Don't explain the joke.

## 5. How kids take in a moral
Research by Darcia Narvaez and colleagues on moral theme comprehension found that children, especially younger ones, often don't extract the theme a writer intended; understanding grows with age into the pre-teen years. Practical consequences:
- **Make the good act concrete and visible**: a character does a specific thing (brings bread) on screen. Kids imitate what they see a liked character do more than what they're told.
- **State the landing plainly**, in story language, not as a rule. "It only measured who was watching" works; "popularity isn't everything" doesn't.
- **Give the kid a question to carry.** The end card turns a story into a thought about their own life, which is where values form.
- **Let the hero be wrong in relatable ways.** A kid who recognises themselves in the hero's mistake learns more than one watching a perfect role model.
- **Repetition across episodes.** The channel's values add up through the corpus; one episode doesn't need to carry everything.

## 6. Voice and narration
- First person, calm, slightly wry, like telling a friend something you only just understood.
- Short sentences. Plain words a 9-year-old reads easily. One idea per line.
- Mark pauses with `/`. About 150 words a minute, so a 70-second episode is roughly 170 to 190 words.
- Numbers and invented rules are said exactly the same way each time, so they become catchphrases.
- The landing line is the slowest line in the episode.

## 7. Worked example (EP01: Everyone Has a Number)
- Everyday thing: follower and like counts.
- Myth: visible approval measures your worth.
- Literal world: a number floats over every head.
- Escalation: the hero performs; the number goes 900 → 3,000 → 10,000 while the square empties.
- Turn: the bread stops coming; the baker (number 9) is sick, and no number showed it.
- Sideways lesson: the number only measured who was watching; the good that matters is mostly unseen.
- Landing reverses the hook: "Turns out the number never measured what we thought."
- End card: "What's something good you did that nobody saw?"

## 8. Idea bank to start from
Seeds only; each still needs a myth decode and a sideways lesson.
- **Sorry Tax**: every apology costs 10 coins. The richest kid has never apologised and has no friends left to owe.
- **The Cool Fade**: kids who act like they don't care slowly turn see-through. The hero is almost invisible when someone needs help.
- **Trophy House**: winning any argument spawns a trophy. The hero's house is so full of trophies there's no room for guests.
- **Main Character Spotlight**: a spotlight follows whoever thinks they're the main character. The hero finally gets it, and realises everyone else is in the dark.
- **The Mood Weather**: everyone's mood shows as personal weather. The hero hides their raincloud with an umbrella, and so does everybody else.
- **Inbox Zero**: the hero can mute anyone in real life. The town gets very quiet.
- **Price Tags**: everyone sees price tags on everything, including friendships. The baker's bread is marked "free" and nobody trusts it.
